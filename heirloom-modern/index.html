The Piece

Closed, it is a room's quiet centre. Open, it is a reason to call people.

Most dining tables are furniture. This one is infrastructure — the thing a family organises itself around for forty years without ever quite noticing.

It was built by the John Widdicomb Company of Grand Rapids, Michigan, in the years when American furniture was at the very top of its game and hadn't yet learned to cut corners. The top is warm honey-toned walnut with a quiet, even figure — the kind of restraint that reads as expensive rather than loud. It sits on two sculpted walnut pedestals, each rising to a slim waisted column and splaying into four legs that end in the original brass levelling glides.

The company that made it had, by then, been drawing its own furniture for half a century under one man — Ralph Widdicomb, who took first prize in Paris in 1900 and stayed at the board until 1951. This table sits right at the end of that run.

Three leaves came with it and three leaves are still here, which is rarer than the table itself. Leaves get lost. Leaves get left in basements and garages and estate sales. These didn't.

The Maker

A hundred and forty-five years of one family's name on the underside of the furniture.

The Widdicomb story starts with an English-trained cabinetmaker stepping off a boat in 1845 and ends in 2002, when the last company bearing the name closed its doors. In between: a civil war, four sons, a canal-side workshop, and some of the finest furniture ever produced in the United States.

Grand Rapids was the Detroit of furniture

George Widdicomb trained as a cabinetmaker in England, emigrated in 1845, and set up first in Syracuse, New York. But the hardwood forests of Michigan and the river running through Grand Rapids were pulling every furniture man in the country west. He followed them.

By 1857 he had opened a cabinet shop with his four sons — William, Harry, John and George Jr. — and his English training showed. Widdicomb work was simply better made than what surrounded it, and buyers noticed.

Then the war took all four sons at once. George carried the shop alone until it failed in 1864. What happened next is the part worth remembering: the boys came home, and they started again.

  • 1897

    John Widdicomb strikes out on his own

    He leaves the family firm to found The John Widdicomb Company — the maker of this table — building a reputation on fine case work and refined period design.

  • 1898

    Ralph Widdicomb takes the drawing board

    Harry's nephew, Ralph H. Widdicomb, joins the new company as its designer — and stays fifty-three years, drawing every piece John Widdicomb produced until he retires in 1951.

  • 1900

    First prize at the Paris Exposition

    Ralph's American Empire mahogany bedroom suite takes first prize in Paris. A Grand Rapids shop, judged best in the world.

  • 1910

    John dies; his son Harry takes over

    The company stays in the family and keeps its standards.

  • 1940s–50s

    The modern era, and this table

    The Widdicomb firms attract the best designers in America — T.H. Robsjohn-Gibbings, George Nakashima, and John Widdicomb's own J. Stuart Clingman, whose sculpted walnut dining furniture defines the company's mid-century look. A table of this date falls at the end of Ralph Widdicomb's fifty-three years at the drawing board, and the beginning of Clingman's.

  • 2002

    The name retires after 145 years

    Widdicomb closes. The John Widdicomb designs are acquired by Stickley. Nothing further is made — which is why what survives is what there is.

Why It Was Built This Way

Three things a factory today would not do.

The top

A top that stayed flat

Seventy years of seasons, three leaves in and out, and the top still closes tight with no step at the seams. That is what properly dried, properly built walnut does. It is also the first thing to fail on a cheap table, which is why it is the first thing to check on an old one.

The base

Pedestals shaped from solid stock

Each base is a waisted column carved from solid walnut, splaying into four slim legs on the original brass levelling glides. No veneer over a core, no stamped metal. It costs more material and far more hand finishing — and it is why nothing here wobbles.

The leaves

Watertown steel slides

The extension runs on galvanised steel slides stamped Watertown Table Slide Corp., Watertown, Wisconsin — the firm that invented the wooden table slide in 1886 and supplied the best American furniture makers. Widdicomb bought the good hardware, and it still glides.

Evidence

How you date a table without a receipt.

Most vintage furniture is sold on a guess and an adjective. This one can be pinned down, because whoever built it bought good hardware and stamped it.

The slides put a floor under the date

The Watertown Table Slide Corporation invented the wooden table slide in 1886 and spent the next century supplying the best furniture houses in America. They did not introduce a galvanised steel slide until 1957.

These slides are steel, and they are stamped patent pending — the marking a manufacturer uses in the early life of a design, before the patent issues. So this table was built in 1957 or after, and most likely within a few years of it.

That is not a dealer's estimate. It is stamped into the hardware, and you can read it yourself with a flashlight.

And a number, branded into the wood

Under the apron, burned rather than printed, is the figure 30 — a factory batch or model number of the kind Grand Rapids shops used to keep runs straight on the floor.

It is worth saying plainly what this does and does not prove. It confirms a factory piece from a shop that numbered its work. The attribution to John Widdicomb rests on the form: the boat-shaped top, the waisted splayed-leg pedestals, the brass levelling glides, and chairs that match the company's known mid-century line. Come look at the underside in person. That is how a piece like this should be bought.

Questions

What buyers ask.

How large does it get with all three leaves?

Closed it seats six comfortably. With all three leaves in it runs to roughly ten feet and seats ten to twelve. Exact figures are being measured against a tape and will be posted here — ask and you'll have them the same day.

How do you know it's a John Widdicomb, and how do you date it?

The form — the boat-shaped top, the waisted splayed-leg pedestals, the brass glides — is characteristic John Widdicomb work of the period. The underside carries a branded factory number, 30. The tightest evidence is the hardware: the extension runs on galvanised steel slides stamped Watertown Table Slide Corp., patent pending, and Watertown did not introduce steel slides until 1957 — so the table cannot pre-date that year. Come see the underside yourself; it is the honest way to buy a piece like this.

Has it been refinished?

No. It wears its original factory finish, with the light marks and softening you would expect from a table that has been used for sixty years. That is a choice: an original surface is worth more to most collectors than a stripped and re-sprayed one, and it can always be refinished later. It cannot be un-refinished.

Will it fit my dining room?

Allow 36 inches of clearance around the table for chairs and walking room. Closed it suits most ordinary dining rooms. Fully extended it wants a long room — plan on roughly 17 feet by 10. Most owners run it short all year and open it for the holidays.

What condition is it in?

Structurally excellent and cosmetically honest. The pedestals, apron and all three leaves are sound, the slides run smoothly and the top closes tight at every seam. There are light surface marks on the top from ordinary use. Close-up photographs of any area you want to see are available before you commit.

Can I see it before buying?

Yes, and you should. It is viewable by appointment in Colorado Springs.
